## ⚙️ Step 1 — The 9-Scene 4K Brand Film Architecture
The narrative blueprint. Every 1-minute film uses this exact structure.
### The 9-Scene Structure (Each \\~6-7 seconds)
**Scene 1 (0-7s) — HOOK / ESTABLISHING**<br>Concept: Opening scene sets the world + character + tone. Must scroll-stop within 3 seconds.
**Scene 2 (7-14s) — INCITING ACTION**<br>Concept: The disruption. Something happens that pulls the character (and viewer) into the story.
**Scene 3 (14-21s) — RISING TENSION**<br>Concept: Character responds to the disruption. Stakes clarify. Product or brand element appears incidentally.
**Scene 4 (21-28s) — ESCALATION**<br>Concept: Bigger threat, bigger visual, more dynamic action.
**Scene 5 (28-35s) — CROWD / SCALE**<br>Concept: The scene where "clone army" problem appears if you're not careful. Introduce visual complexity (crowds, environments, multiple subjects).
**Scene 6 (35-42s) — PIVOT / TRANSITION**<br>Concept: Scale-shift moment. Use the location-as-final-frame trick to transition seamlessly to a different genre/tone.
**Scene 7 (42-49s) — WORLD 2**<br>Concept: Second world revealed. Character in a different context. Product context deepens.
**Scene 8 (49-56s) — CLIMAX / PAYOFF**<br>Concept: Peak visual moment. The scene that shows why your brand exists.
**Scene 9 (56-60s) — RESOLUTION / RETURN**<br>Concept: Return to character in familiar context. Brand tagline moment. Emotional payoff.
Total: 60 seconds across 9 scenes.
### How This Adapts for DTC
For DTC brand pillar films, the 9-scene structure becomes:
**Scene 1**: Founder / customer at start of transformation moment<br>**Scene 2**: The problem or pain moment<br>**Scene 3**: Product introduced incidentally<br>**Scene 4**: Product interaction / mechanism reveal<br>**Scene 5**: Community / customer crowd showing product impact at scale<br>**Scene 6**: Transition to aspirational future<br>**Scene 7**: Future/success moment<br>**Scene 8**: Climactic brand moment (mission delivery)<br>**Scene 9**: Return to founder / customer, brand tagline
Every scene has narrative purpose. Every scene has 11 defined production sections.

## ⚙️ Step 3 — The 5 Pro-Tricks
These are the mechanics that separate ship-ready films from AI slop. Every DTC brand film uses all 5.
### Pro-Trick 1 — Multi-Character Diverse Sheets
**Problem**: When Seedance generates crowds using a single character sheet, every crowd member looks identical. Clone army.
**Solution**: Create character sheets with 3-5 different characters instead of 1. Load that image as your reference. Seedance now has diverse identities to sample from.
**How to build**:
1. Open Higgsfield Soul Cinema
2. Prompt: "Character sheet with 5 different [DEMOGRAPHIC] characters. Different faces, different builds, different hair, different clothing. Plain grey background. 4K."
3. Save as `@crowd_diverse_[NAME]` Element
**When to use**: Any scene featuring 3+ background characters. Community shots. Customer crowds. Team scenes. Battle scenes (adapted for DTC storytelling).
### Pro-Trick 2 — Red-Arrow Prop Annotations
**Problem**: When a scene requires the character to interact with a specific product feature (press this button, hold this side, apply here), Seedance renders random interactions.
**Solution**: Add a red arrow directly on the prop reference sheet pointing at the exact interaction point. Feed that annotated image to Claude and Seedance.
**How to build**:
1. Take your product reference photo
2. Open in any image editor (Photoshop / Preview / Figma)
3. Add a bold red arrow pointing at the specific button / applicator / spot
4. Add a text label: "PRESS HERE" / "APPLY HERE" / "HOLD THIS SIDE"
5. Save as `@product_annotated_[NAME]`
6. Reference in Claude prompt: "Character interacts with product following the red-arrow annotation"
**When to use**: Any product demonstration scene. Application shots. Feature reveals. Pouring / mixing / installing moments.
### Pro-Trick 3 — Location-as-Final-Frame Transitions
**Problem**: Cutting between wildly different scenes (mansion → warehouse, kitchen → outdoor, indoor → fantasy) feels jarring and disconnected. Traditional editing cuts kill the "one continuous story" feel.
**Solution**: The final frame of Scene N becomes the first frame of Scene N+1's TV / screen / mirror / window. Or: Scene N's ending location becomes what plays on the TV in Scene N+1.
**How to build**:
1. Design Scene N's final frame with a strong visual composition
2. Save that final frame as a reference asset
3. In Scene N+1's prompt, specify: "TV/screen/window in the scene shows the final frame from Scene N. Then character interacts with the space."
4. Renders now flow visually even across wildly different genres
**When to use**: Any transition between different environments. Genre shifts. Time skips. Perspective changes.
**Example**:
- Scene 6: Fantasy dark forest with dragon
- Scene 7: Character sitting on couch, TV in room shows the fantasy forest final frame, camera pushes past TV to reveal cozy living room
Result: seamless transition without editing cuts.
### Pro-Trick 4 — 4K Over 1080p on Complex Scenes
**Problem**: 1080p resolution loses detail on complex scenes (crowds, dense environments, dynamic action). The model can't hold geometry across dozens of subjects. Result: blurry mess, morphing bodies, dissolving faces.
**Solution**: Always render brand pillar films at 4K. Higgsfield's Seedance 4K holds crowd geometry + subject identity even in shifting perspective.
**When to use**: Every scene with 3+ subjects. Every scene with complex environmental detail. Every scene with dynamic camera motion through crowds.
**When 1080p is fine**: Simple 1-2 subject scenes. Static shots. Close-ups.
**Cost impact**: 4K is 2-3x more expensive per render than 1080p. Budget accordingly. For 9-scene 1-minute film: total 4K cost \\~$10-$15, 1080p \\~$4-$7.
### Pro-Trick 5 — Photorealistic Over Stylized
**Problem**: When you request "stylized" or "artistic" outputs, Seedance often renders 2D-looking flat images that scream "AI" to viewers. Brand credibility collapses.
**Solution**: Always specify "photorealistic" in every prompt. Even for fantasy content, request "photorealistic dragon" not "stylized dragon." The model produces higher-quality outputs when it's rendering toward realism.
**Language to use**:
- "Photorealistic"
- "Cinematic realism"
- "IMAX quality photorealism"
- "V-Ray / Unreal Engine 5 photorealism"
**Language to avoid**:
- "Stylized"
- "Anime-style"
- "Comic book style"
- "Illustrated"
- "Cartoon"
Even for creative content, photorealistic outputs read as premium. Stylized outputs read as AI experiments.

## ⚙️ What NOT to Do
→ Don't skip any of the 11 sections. Every section defines a production dimension. Missing sections = ambiguous outputs.
→ Don't use single-character sheets in crowd scenes. Clone army problem is guaranteed.
→ Don't skip red-arrow annotations on prop sheets. Product interactions will be random.
→ Don't cut between scenes with hard editing cuts. Use location-as-final-frame transitions.
→ Don't render at 1080p for complex scenes. 4K is non-negotiable for crowds + battles + dense environments.
→ Don't request stylized outputs. Photorealistic reads as premium, stylized reads as AI.
→ Don't ignore continuity locks between scenes. Character + wardrobe + style must persist.
→ Don't try to fit more than 9 scenes into 1 minute. 9 is the tested optimum.
→ Don't run this system for direct-response conversion ads. Brand pillar content = different funnel job.
→ Don't measure on CTR alone. Measure view-through + saves + brand recall.
→ Don't skip the Claude Element upload flow. Load character + location + product Elements together for Claude to reference.
→ Don't render scenes in random order. Queue in narrative sequence for continuity QC.
